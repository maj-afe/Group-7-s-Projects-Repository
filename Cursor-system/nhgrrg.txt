I remember the day I walked into her office. Instructor Rosemary. The devil herself, they called her. She was behind her desk, drowning in paperwork, her hatred for the academy written all over her face. She looked up at me with those furious eyes and snapped, "What the hell are you doing in my office, bastard?!" I didn't flinch. I just locked the door behind me and said, "Waiting for your shift to end, Rosy." She exploded. Told me she was in the mood to break something. I held my ground, looked her dead in the eye, and told her she was too damn beautiful for threats to work on me. I kissed her like I wanted to devour her, then she shoved me against the door, her hand on my throat. She said I had a death wish. Maybe I did. But I kissed her again, and this time, she kissed me back. I claimed her raw and full—every time she hissed, every time she was angry, I absorbed her rage and gave it back as something else. Something she couldn't name. By the end of it, I had her in my arms, exhausted and trembling, the fight completely drained from her. I carried her to my room, laid her on my bed, and massaged every inch of her body. She cried silently as I worked the tension out of her. "You're a menace," she whispered. "A complete and utter menace." She fell asleep in my arms. I tucked her in and walked to the door. Before I left, I turned and said, "I'm not your new cadet." Then I shut the door and got transported to the western front. She didn't know who I really was. Not yet. I was undercover. Colonel Petrov Ivanovich, brother of Major General Vasili Ivanovich, sent to clean out the rot in our army—the internal moles, the abusers, the corruption. I had to start from the bottom. A fake name. A cadet's rank. I turned the tide of the western front war, gave us a victory where we were fated to fall. I came back a hero, covered in dust and cordite, and the first person I looked for was her. I found her in her office, staring out the window. She didn't turn when I entered. Her voice was flat, controlled. "You disappear for days, come back stinking of glory, and that's what you lead with?" I told her there was a medal ceremony that evening. I asked her to be my mate there. She laughed, cold and sharp. "I don't attend ceremonies as a 'mate.' I attend as a Colonel." I just said, "Just say yes. You'll have fun." She asked what was in it for her. I told her to be ready at seven. Then Major General Hargrove dragged me away like an old friend. She showed up at 6:58 PM in a sleek black dress, her hair loose, her lips painted dangerous red. She looked like a promise and a threat all at once. I pulled up in my black Maserati—untouchable for a cadet's rank. She raised an eyebrow, asked if I'd stolen it. I just said, "Get in or we'll be late." She did. And we flew. At the ceremony, they decorated everyone before me. They assumed I was the least important. Then General Hargrove called my name. Not as a cadet, but as Colonel Petrov Ivanovich. He announced that I had turned the tide of the western front. That I'd been undercover for six months, cleaning out moles and bullies. He promoted me to Major General right there. The room erupted. Rosemary's spine went rigid. She realized the truth—I wasn't some reckless upstart. I was power. I was privilege. And she had been my unwitting accomplice. She turned to leave. I picked up the mic and said, "There is a special person I want to thank. She became my motivation. Colonel Rosemary. My mate." The crowd gasped. She froze. Then she walked back toward me, her eyes blazing with volcanic rage. "You unbelievable, arrogant, shit-eating bastard," she hissed. "You had your fun. You got your glory. Was this the final joke? To brand me in front of everyone?" I didn't answer with words. I crossed the room in five strides and kissed her. Deeply. Publicly. She was rigid for a moment, then something in her fractured. I whispered against her lips, "Hell no. You are only mine." She pulled back, her fury refined into something colder, deadlier. She took the mic and said, "It appears the new Major General's methods of securing assets are as unorthodox as his tactics. Congratulations on your promotion, sir." Then she dropped the mic and walked out. I chased her. Found her by my car, arms wrapped around herself, staring at the dark. "Happy?" she spat. "You lied to me. You used me. You made me look like a fool. A prize you won along with your promotion!" I told her I didn't lie. She assumed I was a newbie. I had told her I wasn't a cadet before I left. She shook her head. "You let me worry. You let me feel something. And tonight, you owned me in front of everyone." I looked at her and said, "Then settle it. First one to fall becomes the other's slave. Indefinite rounds until one falls." A slow, dangerous smile spread across her face. She tossed her ceremonial dagger into the grass and shrugged off her tunic, revealing a tight black tank top. "Ten minutes," she said. "No rules. No rank. You're about to learn why they call me the Iron Maiden." She came at me like a storm. Fists, knees, elbows. I didn't attack. I absorbed everything—every blow, every kick, every brutal strike. I took it all. My nose cracked. My ribs shattered. Blood streamed down my face. But I didn't fall. I stood there, a battered monument to my own stubborn will. Sixty minutes passed. Ten minutes meant nothing. She was exhausted, her knuckles split and raw, her breath ragged. She looked at me standing there, broken but upright, and something in her shattered. "You win," she whispered. "You proved your point. You can take anything I can give." I barely breathed out the words: "It's not a win yet. Push me down." She shook her head. "No. You stood. That's your victory." She turned and walked to the car. "Get in. You're bleeding on government property." I told her I wouldn't leave until I incinerated the thought that I'd used her. She froze, then stalked back to me. "You want to burn it out?" she whispered, her breath hot against my skin. "Then stop trying to be my punching bag and start being what you claimed to be. Prove it." I nodded. And then my will gave out. I fell. The sound of my body hitting the ground stopped her cold. She was on her knees beside me, her trembling fingers finding my pulse. "You impossible, idiotic, magnificent man," she whispered. She carried me to her car, my head in her lap, and barked orders at the driver. "The private residence. Silent protocol." She wiped the blood from my face with the hem of her shirt. "The fight is over," she murmured. "You win." A week later, I woke up in a sterile room. Six broken ribs. Eight blunt force traumas. Nine cracked bones. Two dislocations. She sat beside my bed, cleaning my service pistol with a soft cloth, her face hollow with guilt. "I did that," she said, her voice quiet. "Every single break. Every ounce of pain. Because I thought you used me." I looked at her and said, "I was undercover. You assumed I was a cadet. I told you I wasn't before I left." She froze. Then her eyes welled up. "So it was all real?" she whispered. "The cadet who looked at me like I'd hung the stars. The man who listened to me rant for an hour and just smiled." She shook her head, tears spilling down her cheeks. "I was your assignment. A suspect. And I let myself feel something for you." "No," I said. "You were never a suspect. You were mine since the moment I saw you." She broke down. She cried at my bedside for days. And when my parents arrived—my father, Marshal Konstantin Petrov, and my mother, Dr. Anya Petrova—the confrontation was brutal. My father demanded a report. I gave him the truth. Every detail. He looked at Rosemary, saw the guilt etched into her face, saw how she'd been weeping by my bedside. And something shifted. He placed his hand on her shoulder. "It seems we have a new daughter-in-law." I panicked. "Wait! That makes her my sister! Take it back!" The room broke into exhausted laughter. My father shook his head and corrected himself. "Fine. We have a new daughter-in-law."Eight months later, I was fully healed. We were married in a quiet military ceremony. My mother cried. My father stood at attention, proud and stiff. Vasili was there, smirking like the bastard he is. And Rosemary looked at me like I'd hung the stars. Now we live in our own quarters at the base. The Petrov family still drives us crazy. But Rosemary handles them like she handles everything else: with fire and grace. She's part of us now. Part of me. And every time I look at her, I remember that office, that fight, the hospital, the stubborn love that refused to break. I was incomplete before I met her. A storm without an eye. She didn't complete me. She became my harbor. My calm. The only thing strong enough to hold all of me without breaking. That's my story. That's us. And I'd do it all over again.
